Cybersecurity Risk Assessment — Financial Services

Governance, Risk & Compliance

Risk MatrixGRCNIST CSFRisk Register

The Incident

A simulated financial-services organization providing loan processing, check-cashing, and payment services needed its ten core information assets assessed for cybersecurity risk — not a checklist of every possible gap, but a defensible answer to what needed fixing first.

The Mission

Inventory the organization's ten core assets, score each identified risk by Likelihood × Impact, map recommended treatments to the NIST Cybersecurity Framework and CIS Controls, and produce a prioritized risk register.

Before I Started

Which of these ten assets actually carries the highest realistic exposure? Is a 'High' finding automatically more urgent than another 'High' finding, or does the math say otherwise? What has to be fixed in the first 30 days versus the next 90?

Finding — Investigator's Reasoning

Sorting findings by severity label alone would have blurred the distinction between several 'High' risks scoring 15 and the handful of true 'Critical' risks scoring 20. Calculating Likelihood × Impact for all ten assets is what surfaced that the file-server ransomware risk and the Microsoft 365 account-takeover risk weren't just severe — they were also the most likely to actually occur, which is what earned them top priority over other findings with the same 'High' label.

Investigation Timeline

Stage 1
Asset inventory — ten core assets catalogued: workstations, customer database, loan/check processing systems, file server, Active Directory, firewall, Microsoft 365/cloud storage, customer PII, and backups.
Stage 2
Threat identification — relevant threat scenarios scoped per asset (phishing, ransomware, unauthorized access, account takeover, network intrusion).
Stage 3
Quantitative scoring — Likelihood × Impact calculated (1–5 scale each) for all ten identified risks.
Stage 4
Risk categorization — three Critical and seven High risks identified; the two highest scores (20/25) landed on the file server and cloud storage.
Stage 5
Control mapping — each finding mapped to NIST CSF functions (Protect, Detect, Recover) and specific CIS Controls.
Stage 6
Risk register & roadmap — findings consolidated into a prioritized remediation roadmap (Immediate / Short-Term / Planned).

Method

How I Thought
Through This One

Question I Asked

"Given ten identified risks and limited remediation resources, which ones actually go first?"

Evidence

Likelihood and impact ratings (1–5 scale) for each of the ten assets, calculated into a risk score, then mapped against NIST CSF and CIS Controls.

What I Initially Thought

It would have been easy to just group every finding labeled 'High' together and treat them as roughly equal in urgency.

What Changed My Mind

Calculating Likelihood × Impact separated the 15-scoring 'High' findings from the 20-scoring 'Critical' ones — the file-server ransomware and cloud account-takeover risks weren't just severe, they were also the most likely to actually occur, which is what justified putting them ahead of everything else.

Conclusion

A defensible risk register ranks by realistic exposure — likelihood and impact together — not by severity label or discovery order alone.
