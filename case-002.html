Phishing Investigation — Meridian Logistics

Email Security
Incident Response
SANS PICERL
PICERLHeader AnalysisMITRE ATT&CKIOC Triage

The Incident

A Meridian Logistics employee forwarded a suspicious email to the security team. It claimed to be from the IT Helpdesk, warned of a mailbox storage limit, and asked the user to click a link and open an attached "storage report" to verify credentials.

The Mission

Determine whether the message was actually malicious, extract usable indicators of compromise, map the observed behavior to MITRE ATT&CK, and run the response through the SANS PICERL lifecycle rather than a one-off cleanup.

Before I Started

Is this really phishing, or a false alarm? Where did it actually originate? Did anyone click the link or open the attachment, and what would that have triggered?

Finding — Investigator's Reasoning

The email passed a casual glance — the display name and branding read as plausible internal IT correspondence. What gave it away wasn't the content, it was the header chain: the Reply-To and Return-Path pointed to a third domain entirely different from both the claimed sender and the recipient's own domain, and SPF, DKIM, and DMARC all failed together — a combination a legitimate IT notice wouldn't produce.

Investigation Timeline

Step 1
Preparation — raw .eml opened in a plain text editor; link and attachment treated as inert artifacts, never executed.
Step 2
Header analysis — From, Reply-To, Return-Path, and Received-SPF fields compared against the claimed sending domain.
Step 3
Authentication failure confirmed — SPF fail, DKIM none, DMARC fail, all for the same message.
Step 4
URL & attachment triage — link refanged in CyberChef and checked via urlscan.io rather than visited directly; macro-enabled .xlsm flagged as a common payload delivery type.
Step 5
IOC extraction — sender domain, reply-to/return-path domain, originating IP, malicious URL, and attachment hash logged.
Step 6
MITRE ATT&CK mapping — behavior mapped to spearphishing, user execution, and C2 techniques.
Step 7
PICERL response executed — containment, eradication, and recovery actions documented and applied.

Method

How I Thought
Through This One

Question I Asked

"Is a failed SPF/DKIM/DMARC check on its own enough to call this malicious, or could it be a misconfigured but legitimate sender?"

Evidence

Full raw email headers (From, Reply-To, Return-Path, Received-SPF, Authentication-Results) and the declared attachment type.

What I Initially Thought

A single authentication failure isn't unusual on its own — plenty of legitimate mail has SPF hiccups — so one weak signal wasn't enough on its own to call it malicious.

What Changed My Mind

The Reply-To and Return-Path both pointed to the same unrelated domain, different from the claimed sender and the recipient's own organization, and all three authentication checks failed together — that convergence, not any single header, is what confirmed intent.

Conclusion

One weak signal proves little; several independent signals converging on the same infrastructure is what actually supports a verdict. That's the standard I now hold header analysis to.
